# CASE-001 — The Bag That Disappeared

**Publication status:** anonymised product case study  
**Current status:** unresolved  
**Evidence posture:** passenger account and supplied correspondence are not the same as independently verified operational evidence.

## Executive summary

Case 001 concerns an anonymised missing laptop bag after a railway journey involving Frankfurt, Nürnberg and Sonneberg in Germany in October 2026. The passenger and family made repeated reports and in-person enquiries across several locations. Two follow-up messages stated that the bag had not been found.

The case remains unresolved. The available material does not establish theft, disposal, custody transfer, a confirmed train identity, or the bag's present location. RailRecover uses the case to illustrate a coordination problem: a passenger can take many reasonable actions and still lack a shared, time-stamped account of which teams checked which places.

## Passenger actions

The reported actions included online lost-property reporting, police reporting, repeated station visits, an onboard check attempt, police follow-up and a request for CCTV review. The record shows persistence and repeated attempts to obtain an answer; it does not show that every potentially responsible team received, accepted or completed a defined search task.

## Known facts

## Operational observations

The case suggests a need to distinguish several different answers that are often collapsed into one “not found” message:

1. report received;
2. task assigned to a named accountable team;
3. task acknowledged;
4. specified place or scope checked;
5. result recorded with time and actor;
6. item registered as a potential match;
7. ownership and custody verified.

A negative result at one desk is not proof that another location, train, terminal or later inventory will not produce a match. A repeat service number is a routing clue, not a physical-train identifier.

## Unanswered questions

- What did each “not found” response actually cover?
- Was any item registered that could be compared without disclosing sensitive identifying details?
- Was CCTV preserved in time, and was a lawful review approved?
- What retention and deletion rules applied to the passenger's data and correspondence?

## Potential coordination gaps

These are hypotheses for operational validation, not conclusions about Deutsche Bahn's internal systems:

- no shared passenger-visible status describing task ownership and scope;
- unclear distinction between an inventory search and a physical train sweep;
- no visible escalation when an accountable team has not acknowledged a task;
- a general “not found” outcome without a preserved check scope;
- possible mismatch between a passenger's route description and the responsible operational teams;
- no documented handoff record visible to the passenger when an item moves between custody locations.

## Proposed RailRecover intervention

For a future authorised pilot, RailRecover would create one case linked to the operator's existing report and a journey-confirmation task. It would then route bounded tasks to the accountable onboard, station, cleaning or depot teams confirmed by the operator. Each task would record delivery, acknowledgement, scope, result, reason for inability to check and escalation history.

The passenger-facing view would show factual statuses such as “task pending”, “Nürnberg inventory checked — no match recorded” or “potential item registered — verification required”. It would never label an item recovered from a similarity score alone. A found item would remain withheld until ownership evidence and custody handover were verified by authorised staff.

## Current unresolved status

**Unresolved.** No verified recovery event is recorded. No public conclusion is made about theft, disposal, negligence or the present location of the bag.

## Privacy and publication considerations

- This case study is anonymised and excludes names, addresses, personal contact details, police references, ticket identifiers, report identifiers and device identifiers.
- Dates and operational details are described only to the level supported by the source material.
- The case study must not be used to identify the passenger, family members, staff or a specific train.
- A production system would need a lawful basis, role-based access, retention limits, data-subject request handling, secure evidence storage and an operator-approved disclosure policy.
- CCTV, police records and third-party correspondence must not be accessed, copied or displayed by RailRecover without an authorised integration and lawful basis.
- Any public update should be reviewed for re-identification risk and should preserve the distinction between passenger testimony and independently verified evidence.
